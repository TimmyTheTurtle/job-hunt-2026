## Thesis

The lean startup MVP model is a one-bet strategy. You build the minimum thing, observe the
market, then pivot or persevere. Each pivot is expensive — weeks to months, team morale,
investor patience. Most companies get a handful of pivots before something breaks. The model
made sense when prototyping was expensive. AI has changed that calculation entirely.

When building a throwaway proof of concept costs an afternoon instead of a sprint, the
economics of experimentation invert. You don't need to bet carefully on one direction.
You can run dozens of experiments, observe what they reveal, and keep all the information.
Many MVPs. Many pivots. Preserved history. The product emerges from the pattern.

---

## Key Claims

- The traditional MVP model was optimized for expensive prototyping — one bet, minimize waste
- AI reduced prototype cost by an order of magnitude or more
- When experiments are cheap, running many is better than betting carefully on one
- The pivot cost (team morale, investor confidence, time) stays constant even as prototype
  cost drops — which means the many-MVPs model saves the expensive pivots for when they matter
- Preservation of experiment history is what separates this from just "prototyping a lot"

## The Traditional Model and Its Limits

Eric Ries's build-measure-learn loop assumes each cycle is costly enough to warrant careful
hypothesis selection. Steve Blank's customer development is interview-heavy precisely because
building is expensive. Teresa Torres's continuous discovery habits are valuable because they
replace big-bet discovery with ongoing small bets — but still assume each experiment has
non-trivial cost.

When prototype cost approaches zero, all of these models have the same failure mode: they were
designed to conserve something that is no longer scarce.

## What Changes With AI

- A working proof of concept for a narrow idea: an afternoon
- A throwaway pipeline connecting two APIs with a UI: a day
- A demo-able vertical slice of a product hypothesis: a week

At these costs, running 50 experiments over 6 months is not absurd. Running 200 is not absurd.
The constraint shifts from "can we afford to build this?" to "can we read what we built?"

## The Information Problem

Most teams throw prototypes away. The prototype ran. It revealed something. Then it was
deleted and the insight lived in one engineer's head until it was forgotten. Running 200
experiments produces nothing if the information doesn't accumulate.

The many-MVPs model only works if:
1. Each experiment has an explicit hypothesis before it starts
2. The outcome is recorded — what happened, what was surprising, what was revealed
3. The record is preserved in a form that can be queried later
4. Someone reads the accumulated history to derive the product direction

This is the scientific method. It's not new. What's new is that product development can now
afford to run enough experiments to make the scientific method the default workflow.
